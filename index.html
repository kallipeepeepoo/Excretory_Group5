KEY FACT 01

Skin and sweat

The epidermis forms the protective outer barrier. Most eccrine sweat glands sit in the dermis and release watery sweat through ducts to skin pores. Evaporation, not wetness alone, removes heat.

KEY FACT 02

Kidney structure

Each kidney has an outer cortex, inner medulla, and renal pelvis. Nephrons filter blood and adjust the fluid that becomes urine. The renal pelvis funnels urine into the ureter.

KEY FACT 03

Urine formation

Urine forms through glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion. ADH increases water reabsorption in the collecting ducts, making urine more concentrated.

KEY FACT 04

Clinical assessment

Urinalysis can check for blood, protein, glucose, and signs of infection. Urine culture can identify bacteria. Blood creatinine and estimated GFR help assess kidney function, while ultrasound or CT may be used to investigate stones or blockage.

KEY FACT 05

Common skin conditions

Hyperhidrosis involves excessive sweating, while anhidrosis means reduced or absent sweating. Heat exhaustion and heat stroke are heat-related illnesses, and miliaria (heat rash) can occur when sweat ducts become blocked.

LEARNING MODULE

How the body
removes waste.

A lesson sequence for introducing excretion, the urinary organs, kidney structure, and the formation of urine.

01

What is excretion?

Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste produced by cells. It is different from egestion, which removes undigested food from the digestive tract. Excretion helps prevent harmful waste from accumulating and supports a stable internal environment.

  • Carbon dioxide: carried in the blood to the lungs and released during exhalation.
  • Urea: formed in the liver from the breakdown of excess amino acids and removed mainly by the kidneys.
  • Excess water and salts: regulated by the kidneys and also lost through the skin.
02

The urinary system at a glance

The urinary system consists of two kidneys, two ureters, the urinary bladder, and the urethra. Together, these structures filter blood, produce urine, transport it, store it, and eliminate it.

  • Kidneys: filter blood and regulate water, electrolytes, and acid-base balance.
  • Ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristaltic contractions.
  • Bladder: stores urine until urination.
  • Urethra: carries urine out of the body.
03

Kidney anatomy and blood supply

Each kidney is enclosed by a protective capsule. Its outer renal cortex surrounds the inner renal medulla, which contains renal pyramids. Urine drains from the papillae into minor and major calyces, then into the renal pelvis and ureter.

  • The renal artery brings blood to the kidney for filtration.
  • The renal vein carries filtered blood away from the kidney.
  • The renal hilum is the medial entry and exit area for vessels, nerves, and the ureter.
04

The nephron: the functional unit

Each nephron contains a renal corpuscle and a renal tubule. The renal corpuscle consists of the glomerulus, a capillary network, surrounded by Bowman's capsule. The tubule includes the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, and distal convoluted tubule, which drains toward a collecting duct.

  • Glomerulus: filters water and small solutes from blood.
  • Bowman's capsule: receives the filtrate.
  • Tubules: selectively return useful substances and water to the blood and secrete additional wastes.
05

Three steps of urine formation

Urine formation is a coordinated process that changes the initial filtrate into the fluid that leaves the body.

  1. Glomerular filtration: water and small dissolved substances pass from glomerular blood into Bowman's capsule. Blood cells and most large proteins remain in the bloodstream.
  2. Tubular reabsorption: useful substances, including glucose, amino acids, ions, and needed water, return from the tubule to the blood.
  3. Tubular secretion: selected substances move from the blood into the tubule, helping regulate pH and remove certain ions, drugs, and wastes.
06

From kidney to outside

After urine is formed, it passes through collecting ducts, renal papillae, calyces, and the renal pelvis. It then travels down a ureter to the bladder. Stretch receptors in the bladder wall contribute to the sensation of needing to urinate. During urination, bladder muscle contracts and sphincters relax in a coordinated process.

07

Kidneys and homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of relatively stable internal conditions. The kidneys contribute by adjusting the volume and composition of blood and by removing metabolic wastes.

  • Water balance: kidneys adjust how much water is reabsorbed, changing urine volume and concentration.
  • Electrolytes: sodium, potassium, calcium, and other ions are regulated through selective reabsorption and secretion.
  • Blood pressure: the kidneys influence blood volume and release renin as part of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.
  • Blood pH: kidneys help regulate acid-base balance by secreting hydrogen ions and managing bicarbonate.
08

Hormones that influence kidney function

Hormones help the kidneys respond to changes in the body's internal environment.

  • Antidiuretic hormone (ADH): increases water reabsorption in the collecting ducts, helping conserve water and produce more concentrated urine.
  • Aldosterone: increases sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in parts of the nephron. Water may follow sodium.
  • Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP): promotes sodium and water excretion when blood volume is elevated.
  • Erythropoietin: released mainly by the kidneys in response to low oxygen availability and stimulates red blood cell production in bone marrow.
09

The sweat system

Sweat glands are located in the skin. Eccrine glands are widely distributed and produce watery sweat that reaches the surface through ducts. Evaporation of sweat removes heat and helps regulate body temperature.

  • Sweat is mostly water and contains dissolved electrolytes, especially sodium and chloride.
  • When sweat evaporates, heat is transferred away from the skin.
  • Apocrine glands occur in specific regions and produce a thicker secretion into hair follicles.
  • Although sweat contains small amounts of waste, the kidneys are the primary organs for removing urea and regulating the composition of body fluids.
  • Hyperhidrosis: excessive sweating beyond what is needed to cool the body; it may affect the hands, feet, underarms, or other areas.
  • Anhidrosis / hypohidrosis: little or no sweating, which can make it harder for the body to cool down and increase the risk of overheating.
  • Miliaria (heat rash): an itchy or prickly rash that develops when sweat ducts become blocked and sweat is trapped under the skin.
  • Bromhidrosis: noticeable body odour that can occur when skin bacteria break down substances in sweat; hygiene, clothing, and other factors can contribute.
10

How body systems work together

The excretory system does not work alone. It is connected to several other organ systems through blood circulation, breathing, digestion, and temperature regulation.

  • Circulatory system: transports wastes in blood to the kidneys and carries water and solutes between tissues and organs.
  • Respiratory system: removes carbon dioxide, a waste product of cellular respiration.
  • Digestive system: supplies nutrients; the liver processes nitrogen-containing waste and converts ammonia into urea.
  • Integumentary system: skin and sweat glands support cooling and release small quantities of water and salts.
  • Endocrine system: hormones coordinate water, salt, and blood-pressure regulation.
11

Common urinary conditions

Conditions can affect different parts of the urinary tract or the kidneys' filtering structures.

  • Urinary tract infection (UTI): infection in any part of the urinary tract, commonly involving the bladder.
  • Kidney stones: mineral crystals that form stones and may obstruct urine flow.
  • Glomerulonephritis: inflammation or damage involving the glomeruli.
  • Acute kidney injury: a sudden decline in kidney function.
  • Chronic kidney disease: kidney abnormalities that persist over time and may reduce filtering capacity.
  • Urinary retention: difficulty emptying the bladder completely.
12

Keeping the urinary system healthy

General habits can support urinary and kidney health, although they cannot prevent every condition.

  • Drink enough fluids for your needs and activity level.
  • Practice good hygiene and avoid delaying urination for long periods.
  • Follow medical guidance when taking medicines, especially those that can affect kidney function.
  • Manage health conditions with the help of qualified health professionals.
  • Seek medical advice for persistent urinary symptoms, blood in urine, fever with side or back pain, or major changes in urination.
HUMAN–PLANT CONNECTION

Plants remove waste
in a different way.

Plants do not have a separate excretory system like humans. Instead, they use several structures and processes to release, reuse, or store substances they no longer need.

STOMATAROOTS
THE PLANT COUNTERPART

Same general need, different structures.

Like humans, plants produce wastes through metabolism. Unlike humans, they do not rely on kidneys, ureters, and a bladder. They may release gases and water through leaves, release some substances through roots, or store certain wastes in tissues that are later shed.

Important distinction: this is a functional comparison, not a one-to-one organ match. A plant's stomata are not kidneys, and vacuoles are not a bladder.

01

Stomata and transpiration

Small openings, mainly on leaves, allow gases to move in and out. Water vapor also leaves through stomata during transpiration. This releases excess water and helps move water through the plant.

Human comparison: lungs release carbon dioxide; skin loses water through sweat.
02

Storage in vacuoles and tissues

Some waste products can be isolated in vacuoles or stored in leaves, bark, and fruits. The plant may later shed these parts, removing the stored substances from the living plant.

Human comparison: kidneys remove dissolved wastes from the blood in urine.
03

Guttation and shedding

Some plants release liquid droplets through hydathodes at leaf edges, a process called guttation. Wastes may also be stored in leaves, bark, or fruits and removed when these parts are shed.

Human comparison: the body removes wastes through urine, exhaled gases, and sweat.
04

Gas exchange

Plants release oxygen as a by-product of photosynthesis. They also release carbon dioxide during cellular respiration. These gases move through stomata and other openings.

Human comparison: the respiratory system removes carbon dioxide from the body.
Remember: humans have specialized excretory organs, while plants manage wastes through diffusion, storage, shedding, and secretion. Both strategies help organisms maintain internal balance.
